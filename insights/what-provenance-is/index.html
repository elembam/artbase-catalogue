What Provenance Actually Is (and What It Isn't)

Everyone in the art world uses the word. Fewer can say precisely what it means — or what it can and cannot do for you.

The plainest definition

Provenance is the documented history of a work of art: who made it, who has owned it, where it has been shown, and how it passed from one hand to the next, from the artist's studio to wherever it hangs today.

That is all it is — a history, written down and, ideally, sourced. But that modest thing does an enormous amount of work, because in the art world a history is not decoration around the object. It is part of what the object is.

What provenance does

It supports authentication. A work with an unbroken, documented chain of ownership is far less likely to be a forgery or a misattribution. Provenance doesn't prove authenticity by itself, but it is the ground the expert stands on when forming a judgement.

It affects value. The market pays for a clear history and discounts an unclear one. Where the chain is complete and verifiable, buyers, insurers, and institutions treat the work with confidence. Where there are gaps, that confidence — and the value that rests on it — falls away.

It establishes clean title. Provenance is how a buyer confirms a work was not stolen, looted, or illegitimately exported. In an age of restitution claims and due diligence, a defensible ownership history is not a nicety; it is a precondition of a legitimate sale.

It carries meaning. Provenance is also where a work's story lives — the collection it belonged to, the exhibition it appeared in, the moment in history it passed through. That context is part of why the work matters, not only what it is worth.

What provenance is not

Here is where most misunderstanding sits, and where honesty matters most.

It is not a certificate of authenticity. Provenance and authentication are different things. A documented history strengthens the case for a work being genuine, but the judgement itself belongs to a connoisseur or an artist's foundation. A thick file of ownership records is evidence, not a verdict.

It is not a valuation. Provenance informs what a work is worth; it does not state a price. Two separate specialists — a provenance researcher and a valuer — do two separate jobs, and neither should be mistaken for the other.

It is not a single receipt. Provenance is a chain, assembled from many sources: invoices, gallery labels, exhibition catalogues, auction records, correspondence, and — for many artists — the catalogue raisonné, the scholarly master-list of an artist's accepted works. One document is a link. Provenance is the whole line.

It is not always complete — and that is normal. Real histories have gaps. Works disappear into private hands for decades; records are lost; wars and moves erase paper trails. A provenance with honest gaps is more trustworthy than one that is suspiciously perfect. The art market has been burned repeatedly by “improved” provenance — chains quietly forged to raise a work's standing. The lesson is permanent: a recorded gap is worth more than an invented link.

The infrastructure already exists

Provenance research is not guesswork. It rests on real, public tools — the catalogues raisonné that define each artist's oeuvre, institutional archives, and shared resources like the Getty Provenance Index, built precisely so that ownership histories can be traced and checked rather than asserted.

The problem for most privately held art is not that these tools are missing. It is that no one has ever connected a particular work to them.

How Ars Accordia treats provenance

We assemble a work's provenance from its sources and record it — with those sources cited, so anyone can check the chain rather than take it on faith. Where the history connects to a public authority that independently names the artist or the work, we cross-reference it, so the record does not rest on our word alone.

And where the history has a gap, we record the gap. We do not fill it with a plausible guess, and we do not smooth a broken chain into a clean one. That restraint is the point: our value is that the record can be trusted, and a record you can trust is one that admits what it doesn't know.

We do not authenticate, and we do not appraise. We build the sourced, cross-referenced, honest history that the authenticator and the appraiser — and the insurer, the heir, and the buyer — all need to do their own work.

Provenance is the difference between owning an object and being able to prove what it is. Most collections have the first. The record is how you get the second.
