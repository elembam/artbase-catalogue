## Summary
This article frames the scale of the coming generational art transfer: baby boomers own an extraordinary volume of art, and the next generation is not necessarily equipped or interested in stewarding it. The piece argues that the issue is not just sentimental inheritance but a systemic market mismatch: there will be far more art than museums, collectors, or buyers can absorb; many works will either be sold below their emotional value, donated to institutions that cannot take them, or eventually fall into neglect. For Ars Accordia, the article is relevant because it makes the case for documentation, provenance, and stewardship as practical necessities in a world where inherited collections are both large and under-prepared.

# Boomers Are Leaving Behind More Art Than Anyone Wants
Nearly $1 trillion of art is expected to change hands in the coming decade. There won’t be enough buyers or museums to absorb it all.

By Felix Salmon

July 31, 2026 at 8:30 AM UTC

David A. Vaughan occupies a unique position in the history of American modernism. He was born in 1891 in Selma, Alabama, to a wealthy family that afforded him a bohemian artist’s life: He moved first to New York and then to Vence, France, where he founded an art school near the home of his hero Henri Matisse. World War II sent him back to the US, and he landed in Laguna Beach, California, where the light and the ocean reminded him of the French Riviera. There, he taught art, showed his work in local galleries and had one-man shows at the San Diego Museum of Art, the Norton Simon Museum and other institutions.

After his death in 1975, his two children, Sue and David, inherited the bulk of Vaughan’s postwar art — 90 paintings, along with many more drawings and sculptures. They kept the collection largely intact, apart from one piece that was donated to the Los Angeles County Museum of Art in 2011 and a few paintings that were gifted to extended family members. Soon it will end up with Vaughan’s two grandchildren, now in their 50s, who have little ability to look after the remaining works, some of which are extremely good, and are struggling to work out what to do with it. I should know — I’m married to one of them.

The task facing Vaughan’s heirs will be repeated millions of times over in the coming decade, as boomers’ art collections get passed down. Sometimes a collection will comprise a large number of works by a single artist; more often, it will be a diverse grouping of pieces by multiple artists, acquired over many years. Often the amount of time and money spent assembling the collection was huge, and the heirs don’t want to feel it was wasted. But to get any value out of it themselves, they’re going to need to put in a lot of their own work.

Margaret Fisher spent 10 years, from 2014 to 2023, tirelessly working to preserve the memory and importance of her mother, the artist Ethel Fisher. “I’m used to negatively compensated work,” she quips. “I’m in the performing arts.”

Fisher’s project started with building a website, moved on to orchestrating some three dozen museum donations and eventually resulted in a major solo show at LewAllen Galleries in Santa Fe, New Mexico. “They put on an incredible show,” Fisher says. “It was beyond my wildest expectations.” Yet even now most of the work of keeping Ethel Fisher’s legacy alive falls to her daughter, along with an art consultant she’s hired to help her. LewAllen still has many of the 300 paintings that Margaret inherited on consignment, but few have sold.

In a best-case scenario, the family puts on its own private solo show, as painter Eric Tucker’s children did in 2020, two years after he died, in the small English town of Warrington. That show proved so popular that Tucker was picked up by a pair of London galleries, who’ve since successfully managed to sell his work at a series of exhibitions. But stories like that are more the exception than the rule. The sheer tonnage of boomer-owned art is vast, and the total demand for it doesn’t come close to the amount of supply that’s about to arrive.

### The Coming Flood
The baby boomers spent money at an astonishing rate. In the single year of 2005, US boomers spent $7 trillion in 2026 dollars, per McKinsey. Twenty years earlier, in 1985, their predecessors in the Silent Generation had spent just $3 trillion in today’s dollars. Spending on art accelerated as the rich got richer, moved into ever-larger houses and became eager to splash out on discretionary purchases.

That unprecedented demand for art created an unprecedented supply of it too. “The art world became professionalized during the lifetime of the boomers,” notes Michael Darling, a co-founder of Museum Exchange, a company that tries to find museums willing to accept donors’ art. The number of artists making a living from their work reached previously unthinkable levels, with a concomitant increase in the number of galleries. There were about 150 art galleries in New York in 1946, per a 2005 Rand report. That doubled by 1961 and then doubled again by the early 2000s. In Los Angeles, the number of galleries went from almost zero in 1957 to more than 400 in 2005.

When patrons were limited to a few royal courts and religious institutions, the number of people who could make a living by producing art was relatively low. The boomers, blessed with vastly greater numbers, turned their money into battalions of collectable artists. In 1988 the Artnet Price Database, which tracks artists sold at auction, included 8,300 artists, including substantially all the Old Masters. By 2012 there were 90,275.

When you inherit art that was expensive when acquired, it’s difficult to think of it as “stuff” that could be thrown away or given to a thrift store. At the same time, storing and figuring out what to do with it can be enormously challenging. One way of making sure someone appreciates the art is to sell it. But finding buyers can be extraordinarily difficult. Most artists get forgotten over time. Even living artists have a hard time selling their work. A dead artist, like Vaughan, needs a dealer who’s committed to resuscitating interest in them — and those dealers are few and far between, since it’s nearly always easier to sell the work of a living artist than a dead one.

Just looking at items that can be sold for cash on the secondary market, which is a tiny minority of all the art out there, roughly $1 trillion of art is expected to change hands just in the next 10 years, per Deloitte. No one is ready. The great wealth transfer includes a vast quantity of paintings and sculptures. A small proportion of it will have some sentimental value and be kept for that reason, but most of it is going to get moved out one way or another — sold, donated or even trashed.

### What to Do With All the Art?
“We look after about 350 family office collections in 28 countries,” says Philip Hoffman, chairman and founder of the Fine Art Group, which advises families on their collections. “In most of those, the next generation haven’t discussed what they’re going to do with it. Many of them aren’t really interested.”

The problem lies not just with the heirs but also — indeed, mostly — with the collectors. Do the scions even want all that art? Of all the families he deals with, “I can only think of about five that have thought about it,” he says. The rest are in various forms of denial, he says, simply choosing to believe their children would love nothing more than to continue to steward their collection.

To the contrary, as a general rule, “The next gen don’t want it,” Hoffman says. “They want something different.” Selling is quite easy for the 0.1%, who might inherit an art collection worth $100 million or more — something that conservatively happens, on average, at least once a week. Those ultrarich will generally end up being very well looked after by one of the two big auction houses. One of them, Sotheby’s, recently created a Major Collections Division designed to sell art that’s been “hidden away for decades,” as Madeline Lissner, who runs the auction house’s Global Fine Art division, puts it.

“The next gen don’t want it. They want something different.”

For most people, the big houses are likely to be much pickier. There might be one or two pieces they’re interested in, but unless those works are worth millions, they won’t fight for them. For everything else heirs inherit, they can either approach small local auction houses to see what they might be interested in, or, more commonly, sell everything to a professional estate liquidator, who will then move it through various wholesale and auction channels. If there are hidden gems in the collection, there’s a good chance that no one will notice them.

Given the relatively small sums that such sales often garner, many people try to donate much-loved artworks to a museum instead, which at least comes with a tax deduction. The problem is that doing so has become substantially harder in recent years. “There is an avalanche coming,” Darling says. “It’s going to be difficult to metabolize all of that art into museums and nonprofits.”

The old model was that “collectors collect art and museums collect collectors”; high-prestige institutions would cultivate the ultrawealthy, hoping to be bequeathed a priceless collection. Today almost every major museum already has vastly more art than it can possibly display, and, unless it’s offered a masterpiece, it isn’t often hungry for more. At this point, says Rosemary Ringwald, the head of art planning at Bank of America, “anyone who has valuable artwork is aware that the museum might not want it.”

The pandemic savaged museums’ finances: Donors discovered other priorities, ticket sales plunged, staff had to be laid off. Since then, when a museum is offered a donated work of art, more often than not it will ask for a donation of money to accompany it. “Storage, insurance, all of these things are really expensive for museums,” Ringwald says, adding that many museums suffer from aging physical infrastructure too. Filling gaps in the art collection is a nice-to-have; fixing the HVAC system is a must-have, especially as local and federal museum funding continues to decline.

Accepting a donated artwork — cataloguing it, storing it, securing it, insuring it, perhaps restoring it — is resource-intensive. When Ronald Lauder wanted to donate his billion-dollar collection of 20th-century Austrian and German art to the Metropolitan Museum, he also wanted to keep the art in its Beaux-Arts mansion on 86th Street, significantly increasing the associated costs to the Met. It’s notable that when the 2026 deal was announced, the press release listed 27 donors — not including anonymous donors — who together had put together an estimated $200 million to cover the costs of looking after the new assets.

Lacma was kind enough to accept one David Vaughan painting in 2011, but it’s never been shown in public, and realistically is not going to be. The museum has made it clear to our family that so long as the piece, Grim Precision, remains unexhibited, they have no appetite for a second piece. The institutional “priority is to showcase what we have, before taking in new works,” as one of its curators wrote to us, in the kind of email that’s only going to become more common.

### Evolving Tastes
An even bigger barrier for inheritors looking to pass on a collection is the way tastes change over time. Boomers grew up looking at artists like Carl Andre or Balthus, congratulating themselves on how daringly receptive they were to the shattering of historical norms; as those norms get rebuilt, younger art lovers are much more likely to find reasons to avoid showing them entirely. Instead, their collections feature more women artists and tend to be more diverse in many other ways too.

The art world doesn’t fixate on the ultra-new in the way Hollywood or pop music does, where most consumption is of products produced and released in the past year or less. All the same, galleries overwhelmingly show new work by living artists, so that’s what most art collectors end up buying. That means heirs often end up inheriting work that used to be new and exciting but today is often little more than a faint memory.

The rise and fall of artists and artistic reputations is the subject of the emerging discipline of cultural evolution. Just as Darwinian evolution selects for species that do the best at surviving, artistic evolution selects for works that continue to attract our attention. Oleg Sobchuk, a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Germany, explains that art disappears from our collective memory first quickly and then slowly, in a process known as bi-exponential decay. “Sometimes we think about art as speaking to something that makes us human, that it’s general and timeless,” he says. “But in most cases, art is about something that is happening at that period of time.” The further we get away from the moment an artwork is made, the less of our attention it can generally command.

In paleontology there’s the concept of the turnover pulse, where the rate of extinction rises sharply; it’s not dissimilar to the theory of the paradigm shift in the history of science. In art, it’s been more than a century since the modernist revolution that started with impressionism and continued through Picasso and Duchamp. Now, for the first time, the dominant collectors and curators — not to mention artists — will be people who grew up in the age of the internet.

“Every new media, when it appears, creates some kind of rupture,” Sobchuk says. “Usually it functions as a revolution. The culture was in the hands of a small number of powerful individuals, and then from time to time, you have a technological revolution.” That doesn’t mean digital art will displace painting and sculpture. But it probably means there’s less appetite and reverence for modernism. Which doesn’t bode well for those inheriting a lot of it.

### The Slow Decline
In the art world, nothing happens quickly. Even if some boomers are selling, others are still buying. At the big auction houses, buyers older than 60 still outnumber those younger than 40. “People don’t stop once they hit a certain age,” says Sotheby’s Lissner. “They’re continuing to collect as they reach 80, 85, 90.” The oldest American baby boomers are only turning 80 this year; even in 10 years, there will still be about 30 million boomers in their 70s or younger.

Because there will still be many boomer buyers for every collector planning ahead and unwinding a collection, there won’t be nearly as much of a supply-and-demand mismatch over the next decade as the broader generational shift might imply. A lot of their art will simply stay where it is, in ultrasecure art storage facilities around the world. Luxury freeports now cover some 2 million square feet, per Oddný Helgadóttir of the Copenhagen Business School, more than three times what they boasted a mere 15 years ago. Heirs might not like everything they inherit, but if it’s valuable, and if they don’t need the money, and — especially — if the taxman doesn’t know it exists, then the smartest move is often to stay extremely discreet and do any transactions very slowly and very privately.

The great art transfer is a much bigger deal than “what are we going to do with all this stuff.” The art we keep will stay in the canon; the art we discard will be lost to history. All the same, the stuff question is becoming urgent, and its answer is sad, if simple: A tiny proportion of the art we inherit will be treasured and valued, and a little bit more will find a good home elsewhere, perhaps slowly accumulating historical value even if it continues to lose cultural cachet. But most of it will probably acquire signs of physical deterioration and neglect — bumps and scratches and sunbleaching.

David Vaughan’s art looked fresh and new when it was fresh and new, but today it’s showing its age, especially the pieces he painted on Celotex, a construction material that, to put it diplomatically, was never designed to be a base for archival-quality paintings. We’re looking after all of it as best we can, and working hard to find homes for everything. Probably, however, we won’t fully succeed. And if eventually we end up bequeathing it to our heirs — well, I, for one, wouldn’t blame them if they just took whatever was left to a thrift store. All artists are mortal, after all, and only a minuscule fraction of them achieves immortality through their work. The rest are bound to fade away, sooner or later.
